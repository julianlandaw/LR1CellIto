Dynamics of the LR1 Cell with Ito: Pacing, EADs, and Chaos

Analysis of the interactive simulation

October 2026

Abstract

This note analyzes the implemented, periodically paced single-cell model in light of two published studies of memory-induced chaos using the same LR1 and fast Ito formulation. It explains why the default, chaos, and early-afterdepolarization (EAD) presets differ; reports measurements from the JavaScript simulation; and estimates the largest Lyapunov exponent for each preset. The numerical results describe this model and its pacing protocol, not a whole heart.

Model and measurements

The dynamic state is

s = (V, Ci, m, h, j, xr, d, f, xto,s, yto,s, xto,f, yto,f).

Voltage follows the balance of seven membrane currents and the applied stimulus:

dVdt = −(INa + ICa,L + Ito + IKr + IK1 + IpK + Ib + Istim).

Negative net current therefore depolarizes the cell. The rapid delayed rectifier has the form

IKr = gKr xr r(V) (V − EKr),   τxr = tauXfac 1ax(V) + bx(V).

Increasing tauXfac slows the evolution of xr without changing its voltage-dependent steady state. The transient outward current changes the early plateau; itofac scales its fast component and yshift changes its inactivation and recovery curves. The slow Ito multiplier is zero in all three presets, so its two gates evolve but do not feed back into voltage.

The simulation uses a 0.01 ms base step, a −80 stimulus for 0.5 ms each cycle, 10 s of unpaced warm-up, and 10 paced conditioning beats before recording. Action potential duration (APD) is the time from the upward to the downward crossing of −75 mV. Thus the reported APD is a fixed-threshold duration, not an APD90 measurement. The worker counts complete threshold crossings even when an action potential extends into the next pacing cycle.

The chaos multipliers reproduce the published conductances: gto,f = 0.2(1.05) = 0.21, gCa,L = 0.09(1.15) = 0.1035, and gK1 = 0.6047(2.2) = 1.33034 in the model's conductance units. These are the parameter choices used for the memory-induced chaos results in the 2017 paper and Fig. 9 of the 2018 paper [1], [2]. The EAD preset likewise uses the tenfold xr time-constant change studied in those papers, with Ito absent.

Why the chaos preset is irregular

The model has no random input. A beat changes channel gates, calcium, and voltage, which determine the response to the next identical stimulus. If every stimulus captures and stimulus-to-upstroke delay changes little, the diastolic interval obeys the useful approximation

Dn ≈ P − An.

where P is pacing cycle length and An is APD. At P = 378 ms, an APD near 346 ms leaves about 32 ms for recovery; an APD near 50 ms leaves about 328 ms. Slower xr evolution preserves memory of this difference. The strengthened early outward Ito, inward calcium current, and late outward IK1 make the plateau sensitive to the resulting current balance. A small difference in initial xr can then decide whether the action potential rapidly repolarizes as a spike or retains a phase-2 dome and lasts much longer. The papers show that fast Ito sharpens this APD response to xr; the persistent memory comes mainly from slow xr recovery, not from Ito's fast gates [1], [2].

In the JavaScript worker, the last 100 APDs of a 250-beat chaos run span about 50–344 ms, with 98 distinct values after rounding to 0.1 ms. For beats 101–250, initial xr and the corresponding APD have Pearson correlation about −0.73: more available delayed potassium current is associated with a shorter action potential. The correlation is descriptive; APD also depends on the other state variables.

The X-memory map

One can summarize beat-to-beat evolution by a paced map sn+1 = FP(sn), sampled at the same phase of every cycle. A restitution-only sketch would set An+1 = f(P − An). For the published chaos parameter set, the papers found monotonic S1S2 restitution and a monotonic APD dependence on initial xr, yet the restitution-only map missed the chaos seen in the full model [1], [2]. The missing quantity is the X gate carried from one beat to the next.

Let xn be xr at the start of beat n, An = g(xn), and Dn = P − An for one-to-one capture. Approximating an action potential by a depolarized plateau of duration An followed by a resting interval Dn, the gate equation gives the published X-memory map

xn+1 = [xa − (xa − xn)e−An/τa]e−Dn/τd,   An = g(xn),   Dn = P − An.

Here xa is the plateau steady state, while τa and τd approximate X-gate time constants during the plateau and recovery. The formula assumes that the resting steady state of xr is approximately zero. Eliminating xn yields the APD return map

An+1 = HP(An) = g(w(g−1(An), An, P − An)).

where w is the X update above. Even when g and the measured restitution curves are monotonic, HP can be nonmonotonic because APD changes both X activation during the plateau and X recovery during the diastolic interval. A fixed point A* of this reduced map is locally stable when |H′P(A*)| < 1; a crossing through H′P(A*) = −1 gives a period-doubling bifurcation. The two publications show period-doubling routes into and out of chaos for this parameter family, and the X-memory map reproduces those routes. The individual website runs and one-at-a-time changes above show local outcomes; they do not themselves reconstruct the full bifurcation diagram.

For example, the 2018 analysis approximates the monotonic APD-versus-X relation by

g(x) = Amin + Amax − Amin1 + (x/kx)h.

The Hill exponent h controls the steepness of g near kx. A steeper negative g′(x) makes a flip of the return-map slope more likely, while plateau activation, diastolic recovery, and pacing period also enter H′P. Thus increasing memory alone does not guarantee chaos; the competing time scales and APD sensitivity together set stability [2].

Why the illustrative curve misses individual chaos beats

The 2018 paper used two different APD-versus-X functions. Its close comparison with the full LR1 bifurcation diagram in Fig. 9 interpolated a measured g(x) curve. The separate Hill example in Fig. 10 used Amin = 50 ms, Amax = 350 ms, kx = 0.07, h = 25, xa = 0.6, τa = 3000 ms, and τd = 1000 ms, with an illustrative pacing period of 500 ms [2]. The website previously applied those illustrative constants directly at P = 378 ms. Matching the conductances does not make that Hill curve a fitted predictor of this JavaScript trajectory.

We compared the formulas with 250 complete beats from the JavaScript chaos preset. Here xr at upstroke spans 0.06795–0.10585, and 35 of 249 measured diastolic intervals are under 100 ms. Upstrokes are exactly 378 ms apart in this run, so replacing P − An with the measured interval does not remove the discrepancy. The paper also reports that recovery of other currents can affect APD appreciably for diastolic intervals below 100 ms [2].

The square-wave plateau update overestimates X at repolarization by about 0.010 on average. By contrast, starting from the measured repolarization X and fitting only exponential recovery gives a next-X RMS error of about 7 × 10−5 on the 99 held-out transitions. Supplying the actual next X and preceding diastolic interval to a locally calibrated APD response gives a 6.4 ms held-out APD error. These checks locate most of the pointwise discrepancy in the illustrative APD response and plateau approximation, rather than in diastolic X decay.

The calibrated map retains the measured X gate at the start of each beat and the actual diastolic interval. It estimates the X gate at repolarization by a cubic-in-APD regression p3(xn, An), estimates recovery by an exponential with fitted time constant τ̂d, and predicts the next APD by a locally smoothed response ĝ(xn+1, Dn):

x̂n+1 = p3(xn, An)e−Dn/τ̂d,   Ân+1 = ĝ(x̂n+1, Dn).

Writing t = A/350, the plateau regression is p3(x, A) = x Σk=03 bk tk + Σk=13 ck tk. The response estimate is a Gaussian-weighted average of observed APDs,

Kj(x, D) = exp[−½((x − xj)/hx)2 − ½((D − Dj)/hD)2],   ĝ(x, D) = Σj Kj(x, D)AjΣj Kj(x, D).

The fit uses the first 60% of complete beats; the remaining transitions are held out. The bandwidths hx and hD are chosen by leave-one-out prediction within the fitting portion. Fitted X recovery is about 1163 ms, close to the paper's approximate 1000 ms. The main pointwise errors arise from the illustrative g and square-wave plateau update, while the recovery approximation is comparatively good. These are one-step predictions using the measured current beat, on the same run and at the same parameters. They do not establish a parameter-independent return law or predict an entire chaotic trajectory without re-observation.

Largest Lyapunov exponent

The largest Lyapunov exponent measures the average exponential growth rate of a small difference in initial state under identical forcing. For the paced map and a generic infinitesimal perturbation, its definition is

λbeat = limN→∞ 1N log ∥D(FPN)(s0) δs0∥∥δs0∥.

A positive value means nearby trajectories separate on average; a negative value means they converge. We estimated it with two identically paced cells, measuring their separation dn+1 once per cycle and rescaling it to ε = 10−6 after each measurement:

λ̂beat = 1N Σn=0N−1 log dn+1ε,   λs−1 = λbeatP in seconds.

The separation norm scales voltage by 100 mV, intracellular calcium by 10−4 mM, and each gate by one. The initial perturbation was applied to xr; subsequent rescaling followed the full state difference. Both trajectories receive the same stimuli. Estimates were calculated in the JavaScript model after the standard warm-up and conditioning protocol.

For the chaos preset, e0.33 ≈ 1.39, so a small state perturbation grows by about 39% per beat on average; its e-folding time is about 1/0.33 ≈ 3 beats. The positive sign also survived a 100-fold change in perturbation size and halving or doubling the time step in a matching C++ implementation. These checks support a chaotic interpretation of the implemented paced dynamics, but a finite-run estimate is not a mathematical proof about the continuous equations. The displayed EAD preset has only 15 beats; that plot establishes an EAD, while the positive EAD exponent comes from a separate 200-beat calculation.

How the EAD forms

The EAD preset increases only tauXfac to 10 and uses a long 1575 ms pacing cycle. Slower xr evolution delays outward IKr during a long plateau. On the twelfth recorded beat, the inward L-type calcium current strengthens enough to reverse the net current, causing a secondary voltage rise before final repolarization. In the published LR1 analysis, this same tenfold X-gate change produces EADs and a period-doubling transition near this pacing range [1], [2].

The net current changes from slightly outward to inward between 725 and 850 ms. Because dV/dt = −Inet, that sign change accounts for the renewed depolarization. A longer APD alone would not establish an EAD; the secondary rise in voltage does.

The website's EAD counter uses an explicit detection rule: at least 100 ms after the upstroke, voltage must rise by at least 5 mV from a trough at or below −15 mV. It counts only completed APDs and rearms after a 5 mV fall. This operational count helps locate secondary rises for inspection; it is not a separate dynamical classification.

The papers also report staircase-shaped APD restitution in the EAD regime: each higher step corresponds to another EAD. A restitution-only map misses the observed period-doubling sequence, while a map that retains X-gate memory reproduces it [1], [2]. This is a second way that the slow gate changes the outcome, alongside the directly measured current reversal above.

Interpretation and next calculations

This simulation is a single cell with fixed sodium and potassium concentrations, simplified intracellular calcium dynamics, no tissue coupling, and no stochastic channel noise. It can reveal nonlinear mechanisms within its equations, but its exponents are not clinical risk estimates. The 2018 paper also studies a distinct calcium-accumulation memory mechanism in a ten Tusscher human ventricular model; that model and its calcium-memory map are not implemented on this website [2]. The website offers a finite pacing-cycle-length sweep of late APDs and completed-APD ratio, a measured APD-versus-X plot, and a measured APD return map. For the published chaos conductances with one-to-one capture, it shows held-out predictions from the calibrated X-memory map. These interactive scans are exploratory. Denser bifurcation diagrams with longer transients, a tauXfac sweep, and the full Lyapunov spectrum from repeated Jacobian or perturbation propagation with QR orthogonalization remain future calculations. The spectrum could help estimate attractor dimension; it has not been calculated here.

The original Luo–Rudy study reported periodic and aperiodic responses under pacing [3]. The two Landaw papers supply the published X-memory mechanism and bifurcation results for closely matching LR1-with-Ito and EAD settings [1], [2]. The Lyapunov values and current samples in this note are separate measurements from this repository's JavaScript simulation.
